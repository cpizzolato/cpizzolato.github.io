About

The Pizzolato Lab builds personalised, physics-based models of the human neuromusculoskeletal system that run fast enough to close a control loop, and uses them to drive devices that restore movement. The models take measured muscle activity and motion as input, estimate the forces that muscles, tendons, and joints experience during real tasks, and return those estimates to the person, to a stimulator, or to a robot within milliseconds. Combining these mechanistic models with artificial intelligence supports causal understanding of injury and disease rather than prediction alone.

The laboratory sits within the Australian Centre for Precision Health and Technology (PRECISE) and the Advanced Design and Prototyping Technologies Institute (ADAPT) at Griffith University, on the Gold Coast. Associate Professor Claudio Pizzolato leads the laboratory and the BioSpine program. He holds a PhD from Griffith University and degrees in mechatronics engineering from the University of Padua, and is the original author of CEINMS and RTOSIM, two open-source toolboxes used by biomechanics groups internationally.

Research

  • BioSpine: restoring movement after spinal cord injury. BioSpine combines a brain-computer interface, functional electrical stimulation, transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation, virtual reality, and a pharmacological adjunct into one closed loop, with a digital twin of the participant coordinating stimulation so that it matches the movement the person intends. Participants evoke cycling motor imagery, the interface decodes that intent from the electroencephalogram, and stimulation drives the legs on an instrumented ergometer while the virtual environment returns congruent visual and auditory feedback. Efficacy studies have run continuously in the BioSpine Hub at Griffith University since 2021, with more than twenty participants with spinal cord injury. After a year of training, individuals with chronic complete injury partially recovered sensorimotor and autonomic function and showed increased bone mineral density. BioSpine 3.0, funded by the Motor Accident Insurance Commission and Griffith University from 2026 to 2031, moves the technology out of the university and into clinics and homes.
  • Real-time neuromusculoskeletal modelling. Electromyogram-informed models estimate musculotendon forces and joint moments from measured excitations, with no assumption about how the nervous system recruits muscle. CEINMS, released open source in 2015, established the approach. RTOSIM added real-time inverse kinematics and inverse dynamics to OpenSim. CEINMS-RT, developed with the University of Twente and McGill University, computes joint moments in under 3.1 milliseconds on a Raspberry Pi 2, which is below the muscle electromechanical delay, so a wearable robot can be commanded before the limb moves.
  • Digital twins for precision health care. A digital twin here is a model of one person, calibrated to that person's anatomy and neural control, kept synchronised with measurements and used to decide treatment. Work with colleagues at Griffith extended ISO 23247, the international digital twin standard for manufacturing, to neuromusculoskeletal health care, and mapped two use cases against it: Achilles tendon mechanobiology, and control of a neurorehabilitation system after spinal cord injury. Current work addresses the verification, validation, and credibility evidence these models need before they can inform clinical decisions.
  • Differentiable neuromechanics and machine learning. Auto-differentiable Hill-type muscle models allow gradient-based calibration, which reduced the time to personalise a model by up to twenty-six times while preserving accuracy, and let muscle synergies synthesise the excitations of muscles that were not recorded. Current work extends this toolchain with spinal circuit and sensory feedback models, physics simulation on modern GPU frameworks, and reinforcement learning for control of stimulation and exoskeletons.
  • Neural interfaces for the upper limb. NeuroGrip develops an interface that predicts wrist, hand, and finger movement from the proximal muscle activity that survives injury, aimed at restoring grasp after spinal cord injury and traumatic brain injury. The setup combines optical motion capture, surface and high-density electromyography, and force measurement, with neuromusculoskeletal modelling and muscle synergy analysis.
  • Tissue loading outside the laboratory. Personalised multiscale models estimate Achilles tendon strain and joint contact forces during rehabilitation, locomotion, and sport, supporting training prescribed to a mechanical target rather than to a fixed protocol. To move these measurements out of the laboratory, the group develops washable garment-embedded textile electrodes, physics-informed neural networks, and prediction of tendon force from smartphone video.

Software

  • CEINMS, the Calibrated EMG-Informed Neuromusculoskeletal toolbox, Apache 2.0
  • CEINMS-RT, the real-time framework for myoelectric model-based control of wearable robots
  • RTOSIM, real-time inverse kinematics and inverse dynamics for OpenSim, Apache 2.0
